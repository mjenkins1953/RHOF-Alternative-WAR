How credible is this?

Stat Above Average is an alternative to WAR — so the fair test is how it stacks up against WAR, and where it doesn't, whether the reason holds up.

SAA and Baseball-Reference's WAR are answering slightly different questions. WAR measures every player against a replacement-level floor and adds up the wins. SAA measures every player against the league average of the seasons he actually played, and blends a career total with a seven-year peak. The closest thing WAR has to SAA's question is WAA — Wins Above Average — so that comparison matters most.

Across the 1,040 ranked hitters with a comparable Baseball-Reference record, SAA lands within a point or two of what WAR and JAWS say about who belongs. It diverges most on a specific, predictable kind of player — and every one of those disagreements is spelled out below, with the reason.

SAA vs. the establishment

Spearman rank correlation — 1.00 is a perfect match, 0.00 is no relationship. Read down as the pool narrows from every ranked hitter to inner-circle careers.

Broad agreement, loosening as you climb. That's expected: among role players the systems agree on almost everything, but among genuine stars the philosophy starts to bite — a peak-and-rate metric and a career-counting one will rank the same twenty players in a different order. SAA tracks WAA more closely than WAR at every level, which is the metric behaving as designed — it was built to measure above average, not above replacement.

Picking the Hall

Of each system's top N hitters, how many are already in Cooperstown. This pool holds 172 inducted players.

This is the headline result. SAA identifies the Hall of Fame about as accurately as WAR and JAWS do — level with them through the top 150, a handful behind by the top 300, where the last names in are the most arguable anyway. Whatever the disagreements in ordering, SAA is not picking a different set of great players; it's ranking the same ones differently.

The left column is mostly the thesis working as intended: a long career of solid, a-little-above-average seasons banks a lot of WAR but very little SAA, because SAA measures the rate, not the accumulation — and the four bat categories lean 60% on average and power, so a light-hitting dead-ball slap-and-run outfielder scores modestly however long he played. A secondary effect: SAA blends its five z-scores rather than adding runs, so a player who is historic in one category and ordinary in the rest lands closer to the middle than a counting system would put him.

The one everybody asks about

Derek Jeter ranks 145th by SAA, still behind Alan Trammell and Lou Whitaker. Here is that group by every system.

Baseball-Reference's own above-average metric, WAA, also ranks Jeter behind all three — #132 to their #50–58 — for the same reason SAA does: he was a below-average defensive shortstop by every advanced system, and once his bat is measured against other shortstops rather than the whole league, a .310 hitter with modest power is very good rather than historic. An earlier edition of SAA had Jeter down at 238th on a crude home-made fielding metric; moving the defensive input to Baseball-Reference's Fielding Runs — which credit him for playing shortstop even while docking his range — pulled him back to 145th, in line with where JAWS (77th) and WAA (132nd) have him. The ordering was always mainstream; the distance has closed.

What SAA still can't see

No park adjustment. A hitter's home ballpark is not neutralized — Coors Field and the Astrodome count the same as league-average.

The defensive input is Baseball-Reference's Fielding Runs — the one number SAA borrows rather than builds. It's a real, auditable estimate, but it's still an estimate: fielding before the play-by-play era (roughly pre-1950) is reconstructed from limited data and its spread is compressed, and catcher framing before pitch-tracking is inferred. SAA takes it as given and scores it against the league like everything else.

SAA is one lens, applied straight and shown in full — not the last word. Where it disagrees with WAR, the reason is on this page, and you can decide which question you'd rather ask.
