The True Hall of Fame — the same yardstick for every player who ever played.

Ranked on the field, not on ballots.

Welcome to The True Hall Of Fame — a virtual hall for the MLB players whose careers, measured by the statistics they piled up, put them among the best ever to play the game. Membership is decided by a single formula built from box-score facts: a player's Stat Above Average (SAA), the same calculation applied to everyone. There's no five-year waiting period and no requirement to be retired — active players are eligible. Every player here earned the ticket on the field.

The museum in Cooperstown, New York is a wonderful place — artifacts from every era and a gallery of plaques for the players voted in. And that's the operative word: voted. Induction is an honor decided by ballot, with the writers and the era committees weighing each candidate and casting a yes or a no. That's a legitimate way to run a hall of fame, it's the one they chose, and they've never claimed the vote is beyond dispute. But a ballot is a judgment call, and judgment varies — from writer to writer, and from one era's standards to the next. In our view, that has left the results inconsistent enough to be worth a second look on the numbers alone. Some of what that looks like:

Hank Aaron was elected in his first year on the ballot, as he should have been — but not unanimously. He was named on 406 of 415 ballots, 97.8%. Nine writers left the game's all-time home run leader off entirely. Willie Mays drew 94.7%. Ted Williams, 93.4%. You can argue any single omission; it's harder to explain why the inner-circle greats face meaningful opposition at all.

The first player ever elected unanimously wasn't Babe Ruth, Ty Cobb, or Walter Johnson. It was Mariano Rivera, in 2019. Of everyone in the Hall, exactly one has been named on every ballot. One.

And the voting doesn't track production cleanly. Bobby Grich (71.1 career WAR) and Lou Whitaker (75.1) are on the outside looking in, while Harold Baines (38.7) and Tommy McCarthy (14.7) are in.

None of this makes Cooperstown wrong — it's their hall, run their way, by rules they're entitled to set. But in our view the process is broken in a specific way: it's inconsistent, and it shifts with who is voting and when. So we built the other version — one transparent formula, applied identically to every player in history, with no ballots and no committees. That's The True Hall of Fame.
