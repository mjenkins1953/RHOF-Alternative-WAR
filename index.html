Top 200 Hitters by Stat Above Average

This isn't trying to reproduce the actual Baseball Hall of Fame's membership — it's this project's own answer to who belongs. No linear-weight run values, no replacement-level constant: every player is scored only against the actual league average and spread of their own season, using five genuinely separate things you can point to directly in a box score — batting average, isolated power, walk rate, stolen-base rate, and defensive value.

What's a Stat Above Average? Each player-season is compared only to that season's own league average and spread — a 1927 season is judged against 1927, a 2021 season against 2021, nothing crosses eras. The five stats above are each converted to a z-score (standard deviations above or below that year's league mean, computed from qualifying hitters only), averaged into one composite for the season, then summed — not averaged — across every qualifying season of a 5,000+ PA career. Summing instead of averaging is deliberate: a short, dominant peak and a long, steady compiling career should both be able to add up to a real Hall of Fame case, not just the peak.

Negro Leagues players qualify on a separate standard, not the 5,000 PA floor — their official league schedules were far shorter than MLB's, and the surviving record undercounts them further still. They qualify instead by accumulating the equivalent of 9 seasons at their own league's typical season size — the standard that gets Josh Gibson, Willie Wells, and Turkey Stearnes onto this list.

Runs scored and RBI were deliberately left out — both depend heavily on who's on base ahead of you and where you hit in the order, not just your own output. The HOF column marks players already inducted into the real Hall of Fame, so you can see at a glance where this list agrees with Cooperstown and where it doesn't.

How the RHOF Score works

One formula, applied identically whether you struck people out or drove them in.

Why not WAR?

Wins Above Replacement measures every player against one universal "replacement level" — a theoretical bench player, calibrated once from decades of injury-substitution data and then applied identically to a dead-ball-era outfielder and a modern closer. It's the industry standard for good reason, but that one number was never derived for either of them specifically.

The RHOF Score measures every player against their own competition instead — the actual league average, in the actual years and at the actual position they played, blended across whichever eras their career spans.

No replacement level. No runs-to-wins conversion borrowed from someone else's research. Just each player's rate stats, held up against the players who were actually on the field around them.
