Top 300 Hitters by Stat Above Average

This isn't trying to reproduce who's actually in Cooperstown — it's this project's own answer to who belongs. No linear-weight run values, no replacement-level constant: every player is scored only against the actual league average and spread of their own season, using five genuinely separate things you can point to directly in a box score — batting average, isolated power, walk rate, stolen-base rate, and defensive value.

This is a living ranking, not a set of plaques. It's rebuilt from scratch every year on the latest data, so a player climbs onto the list when his case passes the 300th, and a borderline name drops off when someone else's rises past him. Active players are in — no five-year wait. What you're looking at is the 2025 edition; the twenty names just outside the cut are listed at the bottom.

What's a Stat Above Average? Each player-season is compared only to that season's own league average and spread — a 1927 season is judged against 1927, a 2024 season against 2024, nothing crosses eras. The five stats above are each converted to a z-score (standard deviations above or below that year's league mean, computed from qualifying hitters only) and blended into one number for the season: batting average and isolated power at 30% each, defense at 20%, walk rate and stolen-base rate at 10% each. (Walks and steals are both dialed back — a walks-only bat or a pure basestealer could each otherwise ride one category onto the list ahead of a complete hitter; leaning the score on contact and power pulls the list closer to the actual Hall of Fame.)

The career number blends length and peak. A player's season scores, each scaled to a 600-PA workload, are summed into a career total — but a great career cut short shouldn't be buried under compilers, so the ranking metric is the average of that career total and the sum of the player's best seven seasons (the same career-plus-peak shape JAWS uses). Play a long time and at a high level and both halves are large.

Negro Leagues players qualify on a separate standard, not the 5,000 PA floor — their official league schedules were far shorter than MLB's, and the surviving record undercounts them further still. They qualify instead by accumulating the equivalent of 9 seasons at their own league's typical season size — the standard that gets Josh Gibson, Willie Wells, and Turkey Stearnes onto this list. The NeL flag beside a player's rank marks the ones whose score actually draws on Negro Leagues play — a 200-plus-PA season in a Negro Major League — not a brief cameo like Willie Mays's 13 games in 1948.

Runs scored and RBI were deliberately left out — both depend heavily on who's on base ahead of you and where you hit in the order, not just your own output. The HOF column marks players the writers and veterans committees have already voted into Cooperstown, so you can see at a glance where this list and the official one agree and where they don't.

Where the list and your gut disagree

Two it leaves out

Alan Trammell and Derek Jeter are both Hall of Fame shortstops, and the list treats them nothing alike — Trammell is 301st, one name out; Jeter is 431st, a hundred and thirty out. On Trammell the formula basically agrees with the room: a fine all-around career, genuinely borderline. On Jeter it stands alone.

#301 Alan Trammell SS · 1977–1996 · DET

.285 AVG · 110 OPS+ · 2,365 H · 185 HR · 236 SB · 70.7 bWAR

AVG+0.63
ISO-0.12
BB+0.04
SB+0.18
DEF+0.25

A z-line that clears zero across the board and never reaches +0.7 anywhere: a little above his leagues at contact, roughly average power, patience, speed and a slightly positive glove — the shape this score respects, but here it just runs out of seasons at the wrong moment. He is the case for a floating list: one strong year from an active shortstop and he's in; a Negro Leagues data revision and he's out. Right now, 301st.

#431 Derek Jeter SS · 1995–2014 · NYY

.310 AVG · 115 OPS+ · 3,465 H · 260 HR · 358 SB · 71.3 bWAR

AVG+1.32
ISO-0.54
BB-0.12
SB+0.67
DEF-1.02

3,465 hits, five titles, a first-ballot plaque at 99.7% — and the two categories weighted 30% each both go the wrong way. Elite contact (+1.32), but no power for a modern star (−0.54 ISO) and a range metric that graded him a full standard deviation below the line at short for two decades. Twenty years of it doesn't rescue a per-season rate that thin. The formula measures what it measures; here it and the whole sport disagree, loudly: 431st.

At 143 of the 300, this is the closest the list has come to Cooperstown — the expansion mostly pulled in catchers the range metric can't see (Yogi Berra, Iván Rodríguez, Ted Simmons, Gary Carter) and long all-around careers the old 200-man cut left just outside (Lou Whitaker, Craig Biggio, Ichiro). What it still won't do is bend for a profile it genuinely disagrees with — Jeter, Omar Vizquel (896th), Brooks Robinson (334th). That's the point.

On the bubble

The twenty just outside the cut, ranks 301–320. A strong season from an active player, a shift in the Negro Leagues record, or the next weighting pass, and the line moves.

SAA = a weighted blend of five per-season z-scores — batting average [H/AB] and isolated power [SLG−AVG] at 30% each, defensive value [range runs + positional credit] at 20%, walk rate [BB/PA] and stolen-base rate [SB/PA] at 10% each — against that season's league mean and spread among 200+ PA hitters, each season scaled to 600 PA. The ranking number is the average of the career total and the sum of the player's best seven seasons. Pool: 5,000+ career PA (or, for Negro Leagues players, 9+ seasons normalized to their own league's typical season size). Source: Lahman database hitting/fielding (2025 release, including Negro Leagues statistics); Hall of Fame membership current through the 2026 induction class.

Data sourced from the latest version of the Lahman database.
