Top 300 Hitters by Stat Above Average

This isn't trying to reproduce who's actually in Cooperstown — it's this project's own answer to who belongs. No linear-weight run values, no replacement-level constant: every player is scored only against the actual league average and spread of their own season, using five genuinely separate things you can point to directly in a box score — batting average, isolated power, walk rate, stolen-base rate, and defensive value.

What's a Stat Above Average? Each player-season is compared only to that season's own league average and spread — a 1927 season is judged against 1927, a 2024 season against 2024, nothing crosses eras. The five stats above are each converted to a z-score (standard deviations above or below that year's league mean, computed from qualifying hitters only) and blended into one number for the season: batting average, isolated power, stolen-base rate, and defense at 22% each, walk rate at 12%. (Walks are dialed back because a walks-only bat could otherwise ride that one category onto the list while an all-around 18-year regular fell just short.)

The career number blends length and peak. A player's season scores, each scaled to a 600-PA workload, are summed into a career total — but a great career cut short shouldn't be buried under compilers, so the ranking metric is the average of that career total and the sum of the player's best seven seasons (the same career-plus-peak shape JAWS uses). Play a long time and at a high level and both halves are large.

Negro Leagues players qualify on a separate standard, not the 5,000 PA floor — their official league schedules were far shorter than MLB's, and the surviving record undercounts them further still. They qualify instead by accumulating the equivalent of 9 seasons at their own league's typical season size — the standard that gets Josh Gibson, Willie Wells, and Turkey Stearnes onto this list.

Runs scored and RBI were deliberately left out — both depend heavily on who's on base ahead of you and where you hit in the order, not just your own output. The HOF column marks players the writers and veterans committees have already voted into Cooperstown, so you can see at a glance where this list and the official one agree and where they don't.

Where the list and your gut disagree

Two it leaves out

Dave Winfield and Darryl Strawberry are opposite hitters, and the list leaves out both — Winfield 318th, Strawberry 304th. Winfield was above average at everything and dominant at nothing, across 22 seasons; Strawberry had one enormous tool and a career that injuries and off-field trouble cut to eleven. The list runs out at 300.

#318 Dave Winfield RF · 1973–1995 · NYY

.283 AVG · 130 OPS+ · 3,110 H · 465 HR · 223 SB · 64.2 bWAR

AVG+0.59
ISO+1.07
BB+0.28
SB-0.10
DEF-0.90

Three thousand hits, 465 homers, twelve straight All-Star teams, a first-ballot plaque — and a z-line that never clears +1.1 in any category. Above average at contact, power, and patience; a step slow on the bases; a glove the range metric marked below the line. The cannon arm in right that everyone remembers is invisible to fielding percentage — the same blind spot that holds Ichiro's real defensive value down. Steady greatness for two decades, and no towering season: 318th.

#304 Darryl Strawberry RF · 1983–1999 · NYM

.259 AVG · 138 OPS+ · 335 HR · 221 SB · 1,401 H · 42.2 bWAR

AVG-0.09
ISO+2.07
BB+1.17
SB+0.67
DEF-1.46

As pure a power hitter as the 1980s produced — isolated power out at +2.07, one of the biggest single-category z-scores anywhere below the top 100 — with a real eye to go with it. But a .259 average, a corner glove near the bottom of the pool, and eleven seasons. Even with the peak term crediting his best years, one towering skill and not enough of the rest, for not long enough: 304th.

The contrast is Lou Whitaker, 241st — a second baseman a little above average at nearly all five things and never great at any. This list rewards that shape and taxes the specialist, whether the specialty is one tool (Strawberry) or one skill the box score can't see (Winfield). It measures all-around value against your league, not fame or a single towering ability — and Tony Gwynn, who missed the old cut at 310th, now lands 233rd once his eighteen years of elite contact are weighed against his peak.

SAA = a weighted blend of five per-season z-scores — batting average [H/AB], isolated power [SLG−AVG], stolen-base rate [SB/PA] and defensive value [range runs + positional credit] at 22% each, walk rate [BB/PA] at 12% — against that season's league mean and spread among 200+ PA hitters, each season scaled to 600 PA. The ranking number is the average of the career total and the sum of the player's best seven seasons. Pool: 5,000+ career PA (or, for Negro Leagues players, 9+ seasons normalized to their own league's typical season size). Source: Lahman database hitting/fielding (2025 release, including Negro Leagues statistics); Hall of Fame membership current through the 2026 induction class.
