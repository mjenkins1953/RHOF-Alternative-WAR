Top 200 Hitters by Stat Above Average

This isn't trying to reproduce who's actually in Cooperstown — it's this project's own answer to who belongs. No linear-weight run values, no replacement-level constant: every player is scored only against the actual league average and spread of their own season, using five genuinely separate things you can point to directly in a box score — batting average, isolated power, walk rate, stolen-base rate, and defensive value.

What's a Stat Above Average? Each player-season is compared only to that season's own league average and spread — a 1927 season is judged against 1927, a 2021 season against 2021, nothing crosses eras. The five stats above are each converted to a z-score (standard deviations above or below that year's league mean, computed from qualifying hitters only), averaged into one composite for the season, then summed — not averaged — across every qualifying season of a 5,000+ PA career. Summing instead of averaging is deliberate: a short, dominant peak and a long, steady compiling career should both be able to add up to a genuine Hall of Fame case, not just the peak.

Negro Leagues players qualify on a separate standard, not the 5,000 PA floor — their official league schedules were far shorter than MLB's, and the surviving record undercounts them further still. They qualify instead by accumulating the equivalent of 9 seasons at their own league's typical season size — the standard that gets Josh Gibson, Willie Wells, and Turkey Stearnes onto this list.

Runs scored and RBI were deliberately left out — both depend heavily on who's on base ahead of you and where you hit in the order, not just your own output. The HOF column marks players the writers and veterans committees have already voted into Cooperstown, so you can see at a glance where this list and the official one agree and where they don't.

Where the list and your gut disagree

Two it leaves out

Ichiro Suzuki and Sammy Sosa both cleared the 5,000-PA bar without trouble, and to most fans both are obvious. Neither is in the top 200 — the list runs out at 200, and Ichiro finishes 211th, Sosa 217th. That isn't a glitch. It's the direct cost of scoring every player on the same five equally-weighted skills: a career built on maxing one or two of them and skipping the rest doesn't add up here the way it does in memory.

#211 Ichiro Suzuki RF · 2001–2019 · SEA

.311 AVG · 106 OPS+ · 3,089 H · 509 SB · 117 HR · 60.0 bWAR

AVG+1.48
ISO-1.20
BB-0.84
SB+1.76
DEF+0.18

The best pure contact hitter of his era, and the score sees it — batting average and steals both near the top of the pool. But he hit for almost no power and almost never walked, two whole categories deep in the red, and his real defensive value was arm and range, which fielding percentage barely registers. His nine seasons in Japan aren't in this data at all: his record here begins at age 27.

#217 Sammy Sosa RF · 1989–2007 · CHC

.273 AVG · 128 OPS+ · 609 HR · 2,408 H · 234 SB · 58.6 bWAR

AVG+0.05
ISO+1.60
BB+0.05
SB+0.17
DEF-0.42

Elite power (ISO +1.60) and nothing else above the line. League-average bat-to-ball, league-average plate discipline, a below-average glove. The 609 home runs are a counting stat; SAA only sees power rate against a high-offense era, so one great category out of five nets out to a hair above average.

The contrast is Lou Whitaker, 166th — a second baseman a little above average at all five things and never great at any. A formula that averages five categories rewards exactly that shape and punishes the specialist, whether the specialty is power (Sosa) or contact and speed (Ichiro). It's a deliberate trade: this list measures all-around value against your league, not fame or a single towering skill. Ichiro and Sosa are where you can feel the edge of it.

SAA = average of five per-season z-scores (batting average [H/AB], isolated power [SLG−AVG], walk rate [BB/PA], stolen-base rate [SB/PA], and defensive value [range runs + positional credit, same shape as this project's original fielding formula]) against that season's league mean and standard deviation among qualifying hitters, summed across every 200+ PA season of a 5,000+ career-PA hitter (or, for Negro Leagues players, 9+ seasons normalized to their own league's typical season size). Source: Lahman database hitting/fielding (2025 release, including Negro Leagues statistics); Hall of Fame membership from the same release, current through the 2026 induction class.
