Top 300 Hitters by Stat Above Average

This isn't trying to reproduce who's actually in Cooperstown — it's this project's own answer to who belongs. No linear-weight run values, no replacement-level constant: every player is scored against the league he actually played in, season by season and tilted toward his own position, using five genuinely separate skills — batting average, isolated power, walk rate, stolen-base rate, and fielding.

This is a living ranking, not a set of plaques. It's rebuilt from scratch every year on the latest data, so a player climbs onto the list when his case passes the 300th, and a borderline name drops off when someone else's rises past him. Active players are in — no five-year wait. What you're looking at is the 2025 edition; the twenty names just outside the cut are listed at the bottom.

What's a Stat Above Average? Each player-season is compared to its own season — a 1927 season is judged against 1927, a 2024 season against 2024, nothing crosses eras. The five inputs above are each converted to a z-score (standard deviations above or below the bar for that year, computed from qualifying hitters only) and blended into one number for the season: batting average and isolated power at 30% each, defense at 20%, walk rate and stolen-base rate at 10% each. (Walks and steals are both dialed back — a walks-only bat or a pure basestealer could each otherwise ride one category onto the list ahead of a complete hitter; leaning the score on contact and power pulls the list closer to the actual Hall of Fame.) The defensive input is Baseball-Reference's Fielding Runs for the season — the one number here that isn't a plain box-score rate, because fielding doesn't have one.

The bar leans toward your position. A .290 shortstop and a .290 first baseman are not the same player, but scored against the whole league they look identical — and that quietly buried the great up-the-middle names (Ripken, Larkin, Bench) far below where they belong. So the four bat-and-legs categories are measured against a bar set 60% of the way from the league average toward the average for the player's own position that season. Fielding Runs are position-relative by nature — a shortstop's are already measured only against other shortstops.

The career number blends length and peak. A player's season scores, each scaled to a 600-PA workload, are summed into a career total — with below-average seasons counted at 40% weight, so a few years too long in the league dings a great career without erasing it. A great career cut short shouldn't be buried under compilers either, so the ranking metric is the average of that career total and the sum of the player's best seven seasons (the same career-plus-peak shape JAWS uses). Play a long time and at a high level and both halves are large.

Negro Leagues players qualify on a separate standard, not the 5,000 PA floor — their official league schedules were far shorter than MLB's, and the surviving record undercounts them further still. They qualify instead by accumulating the equivalent of 9 seasons at their own league's typical season size — the standard that gets Josh Gibson, Willie Wells, and Turkey Stearnes onto this list. The NeL flag beside a player's rank marks the ones whose score actually draws on Negro Leagues play — a 200-plus-PA season in a Negro Major League — not a brief cameo like Willie Mays's 13 games in 1948.

Runs scored and RBI were deliberately left out — both depend heavily on who's on base ahead of you and where you hit in the order, not just your own output. The HOF column marks players the writers and veterans committees have already voted into Cooperstown, so you can see at a glance where this list and the official one agree and where they don't.

Where the list and your gut disagree

Two it leaves out

Nellie Fox and Don Mattingly are two names most fans would swear are already on a list like this — an MVP apiece, nineteen All-Star nods between them — and the formula leaves both off, for opposite reasons. Fox, 357th, is a Hall of Famer whose whole case is glove and bat-to-ball with nothing else attached. Mattingly, 310th, ran fifteen years on the writers' ballot without getting in; his problem here is the reverse of Fox's — a great bat, and not much career around it.

#357 Nellie Fox 2B · 1947–1965 · CHW

.288 AVG · 94 OPS+ · 2,663 H · 35 HR · 719 BB · 49.3 bWAR

AVG+0.65
ISO-0.94
BB-0.68
SB-0.20
DEF+1.36

The glove is the real thing — +1.36, three Gold Gloves, the best defensive second baseman of his era by Baseball-Reference's Fielding Runs — and he put the ball in play as well as anyone alive, leading the league in hits four times and almost never striking out (+0.65 on average). But there is nothing else. No power at all (−0.94, the bottom of the board), a walk rate below his own league (−0.68), and a park-adjusted OPS of 94 — a career of slightly below-average hitting. SAA leans 60% of the bat score on average and power together, so a glove-and-slap infielder with a hole where the secondary skills go has no way up the list. The 1959 MVP, and 357th here.

#310 Don Mattingly 1B · 1982–1995 · NYY

.307 AVG · 127 OPS+ · 2,153 H · 222 HR · 1,099 RBI · 42.4 bWAR

AVG+1.21
ISO+0.22
BB-0.62
SB-0.61
DEF-0.64

+1.21 on contact — a genuine batting-title bat, and the 1985 MVP — with a touch of power on top. Around it, gaps: he didn't walk much (−0.62), never ran, and the defense grades negative (−0.64) despite nine Gold Gloves, because first base carries a steep positional discount — the glove saved runs, but the bar for the position is low. And the peak, as good as any bat in the game from 1984 to 1989, is basically the career: a back injury turned him ordinary at 29 and finished him at 34. His best seven seasons actually outscore his career total — the sign of a player the years were too few for. A great hitter, a low-value position, thirteen seasons: 310th, and the closest thing on this list to the Hall's own long argument about him.

At 149 of the 300, this is the closest the list has come to Cooperstown. Scoring the bat against the position — and, since the 2025 pass, taking defense straight from Baseball-Reference's Fielding Runs — closed most of the old gap at catcher and up the middle: Johnny Bench (40th), Iván Rodríguez (68th), Alan Trammell (71st), Luis Aparicio (170th) and Ozzie Smith (179th) all sit inside now. What it still won't do is bend for a career built almost entirely on the glove — Bill Mazeroski (722nd), Omar Vizquel (422nd), Rabbit Maranville (436th). That's the point.

On the bubble

The twenty just outside the cut, ranks 301–320. A strong season from an active player, a shift in the Negro Leagues record, or the next weighting pass, and the line moves.

SAA = a weighted blend of five per-season z-scores — batting average [H/AB] and isolated power [SLG−AVG] at 30% each, defense [Baseball-Reference Fielding Runs: range + catcher defense + the positional adjustment] at 20%, walk rate [BB/PA] and stolen-base rate [SB/PA] at 10% each — against that season's mean and spread among 200+ PA hitters, the bar for the four bat-and-legs stats set 60% of the way toward the player's own position. Each season is scaled to 600 PA; below-average seasons are counted at 40% weight in the career total. The ranking number is the average of the career total and the sum of the player's best seven seasons. Pool: 5,000+ career PA (or, for Negro Leagues players, 9+ seasons normalized to their own league's typical season size). Source: Lahman database hitting (2025 release, including Negro Leagues statistics) and Baseball-Reference Fielding Runs; Hall of Fame membership current through the 2026 induction class.
