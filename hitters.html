Top 300 Hitters by Stat Above Average

This isn't trying to reproduce who's actually in Cooperstown — it's this project's own answer to who belongs. No linear-weight run values, no replacement-level constant: every player is scored against the league he actually played in, season by season and tilted toward his own position, using five genuinely separate things you can point to directly in a box score — batting average, isolated power, walk rate, stolen-base rate, and defensive value.

This is a living ranking, not a set of plaques. It's rebuilt from scratch every year on the latest data, so a player climbs onto the list when his case passes the 300th, and a borderline name drops off when someone else's rises past him. Active players are in — no five-year wait. What you're looking at is the 2025 edition; the twenty names just outside the cut are listed at the bottom.

What's a Stat Above Average? Each player-season is compared to its own season — a 1927 season is judged against 1927, a 2024 season against 2024, nothing crosses eras. The five stats above are each converted to a z-score (standard deviations above or below the bar for that year, computed from qualifying hitters only) and blended into one number for the season: batting average and isolated power at 30% each, defense at 20%, walk rate and stolen-base rate at 10% each. (Walks and steals are both dialed back — a walks-only bat or a pure basestealer could each otherwise ride one category onto the list ahead of a complete hitter; leaning the score on contact and power pulls the list closer to the actual Hall of Fame.)

The bar leans toward your position. A .290 shortstop and a .290 first baseman are not the same player, but scored against the whole league they look identical — and that quietly buried the great up-the-middle names (Ripken, Larkin, Bench) far below where they belong. So the four bat-and-legs categories are measured against a bar set 60% of the way from the league average toward the average for the player's own position that season. Defense always worked this way.

The career number blends length and peak. A player's season scores, each scaled to a 600-PA workload, are summed into a career total — with below-average seasons counted at 40% weight, so a few years too long in the league dings a great career without erasing it. A great career cut short shouldn't be buried under compilers either, so the ranking metric is the average of that career total and the sum of the player's best seven seasons (the same career-plus-peak shape JAWS uses). Play a long time and at a high level and both halves are large.

Negro Leagues players qualify on a separate standard, not the 5,000 PA floor — their official league schedules were far shorter than MLB's, and the surviving record undercounts them further still. They qualify instead by accumulating the equivalent of 9 seasons at their own league's typical season size — the standard that gets Josh Gibson, Willie Wells, and Turkey Stearnes onto this list. The NeL flag beside a player's rank marks the ones whose score actually draws on Negro Leagues play — a 200-plus-PA season in a Negro Major League — not a brief cameo like Willie Mays's 13 games in 1948.

Runs scored and RBI were deliberately left out — both depend heavily on who's on base ahead of you and where you hit in the order, not just your own output. The HOF column marks players the writers and veterans committees have already voted into Cooperstown, so you can see at a glance where this list and the official one agree and where they don't.

Where the list and your gut disagree

Two it leaves out

Rick Monday and Bill Terry both land just outside the top 300, and the list leaves them out for reasons that have nothing to do with each other. Terry is a Hall of Famer — a lifetime .341, the last man to bat .400 in the National League — and the formula still has him 304th, because the career was short and first base is a high bar. Monday isn't in anyone's Hall of Fame and never asked to be, yet the same math puts him 301st, one name from the cut, on a batting eye and a center fielder's pop that his .264 average always hid.

#301 Rick Monday CF · 1966–1984 · CHC

A .264 average and 33 bWAR — on paper, nobody's idea of a top-300 hitter. But SAA never treats batting average as the whole story, and it never counts RBI at all. What's left is a real top-of-the-order on-base skill the .264 buried (+1.10 walk rate, among the best of his era), genuine power for a center fielder (+0.79 ISO), and a playable glove up the middle — a 125 OPS+ held for fifteen qualifying seasons. The formula isn't leaving the first pick of the 1965 draft out because it thinks little of him; it has him nearer Cooperstown than the room ever did, and one good year from anyone below moves him in.

#304 Bill Terry 1B · 1923–1936 · NYG

.341 AVG · 136 OPS+ · 2,193 H · 154 HR · 56 SB · 56.5 bWAR

AVG+1.22
ISO+0.34
BB-0.42
SB-0.45
DEF-0.28

The bat is real: +1.22 on contact, a genuine plus on power for the 1930s. Around it, not much — a walk rate below his own league (−0.42), no speed, and a first baseman's glove that grades just under the bar (−0.28). Against the whole league his .341 was a monster; measured mostly against other first basemen, where everyone hit, it's very good rather than historic. And the career is short: twelve qualifying seasons, then he walked away early to manage the Giants. SAA rewards a clean peak — his best-seven and career totals are almost identical, no padding — but there just isn't enough of it. A great hitter, a high position, not quite enough seasons: 304th.

At 149 of the 300, this is the closest the list has come to Cooperstown. Scoring the bat against the position closed most of the old gap at catcher and up the middle — Johnny Bench, Iván Rodríguez, Ozzie Smith and Luis Aparicio all sit comfortably inside now. What it still won't do is bend for a career built almost entirely on the glove — Bill Mazeroski (355th), Nellie Fox (453rd), Omar Vizquel (577th). That's the point.

On the bubble

The twenty just outside the cut, ranks 301–320. A strong season from an active player, a shift in the Negro Leagues record, or the next weighting pass, and the line moves.

SAA = a weighted blend of five per-season z-scores — batting average [H/AB] and isolated power [SLG−AVG] at 30% each, defensive value [range runs + positional credit] at 20%, walk rate [BB/PA] and stolen-base rate [SB/PA] at 10% each — against that season's mean and spread among 200+ PA hitters, the bar for the four bat-and-legs stats set 60% of the way toward the player's own position. Each season is scaled to 600 PA; below-average seasons are counted at 40% weight in the career total. The ranking number is the average of the career total and the sum of the player's best seven seasons. Pool: 5,000+ career PA (or, for Negro Leagues players, 9+ seasons normalized to their own league's typical season size). Source: Lahman database hitting/fielding (2025 release, including Negro Leagues statistics); Hall of Fame membership current through the 2026 induction class.
